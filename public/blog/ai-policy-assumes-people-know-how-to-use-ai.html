Your AI policy assumes people know how to use AI

A policy sets the rules for AI use. Following them takes skill. Here is how to set a baseline for every team, and keep a record of it.

An AI policy tells people what they may do. It says which tools are approved, which information stays out of them, and when a person must review the output. Each of those rules depends on a skill the policy does not teach. “Do not enter confidential information” assumes the reader can recognize it in the middle of a task. “Review outputs before use” assumes they know what a wrong answer looks like.

When that skill is missing, the policy is acknowledged and then missed in practice. The following approach is Alphaworx’s practical guidance; adapt it to your organization’s work, tools, and obligations.

01

Name the skills the policy depends on

Read the policy as a list of things a person must be able to do. For most organizations the list is short: choose an approved tool for the task, keep restricted information out of it, give it enough context to produce useful work, check the result against a source, and know when to hand a question to someone else. If agents are in use, add supervising work that runs without a person watching each step.

Write that list down. It is the baseline: what every person who works with AI should be able to do, whatever their role.

02

Find out where people are

Skill with AI varies widely inside a single team. One analyst has built a working routine; the person at the next desk has tried a chatbot twice. A course pitched at the middle is too slow for the first and too fast for the second.

A short placement check addresses this. Ask each person a few practical questions about how they work with AI today, and place them at a level. Each person then starts from what they already know, and nobody repeats it.

03

Teach it through the work

People keep what they use. Tie each level to tasks from the job: summarizing a contract, drafting a customer reply, reconciling two reports. Shared playbooks help. When a team agrees how a recurring task is done with AI, including what to check before the result is used, good habits spread between colleagues.

Keep one set of safety rules for everyone: what not to share, how to check an answer, and how to supervise an agent. Three rules people remember are worth more than thirty they have to look up.

04

Treat it as a requirement, and keep a record

For organizations within its scope, the EU AI Act makes this an obligation. Article 4 requires providers and deployers of AI systems to take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff, taking into account their technical knowledge, experience, education, and training. The European Commission’s guidance says the article has applied since February 2, 2025, that national authorities began supervising it on August 2, 2026, and that no certificate is needed. It also notes that asking staff to read a system’s instructions may not be effective on its own. The wording of the article has changed since it was adopted, so check the current text and take legal advice on how it applies to you.

Elsewhere the same idea appears as recommended practice. The NIST AI RMF Playbook calls for personnel and partners to receive AI risk management training so they can perform their duties (Govern 2.2).

In either case, keep a simple record: who was placed at which level, what they completed, and when the baseline was last reviewed. A regulator, a customer, or your own board may ask for it.

05

Keep the baseline current

A new approved tool, a new agent capability, or an incident should send the baseline back for review, the same way a change sends a policy back. Assign one owner for the baseline and put its review on the same calendar as the policy.

06

Try this at the next leadership review

Pick one rule from your AI policy and ask: what must a person be able to do to follow it; how do we know they can; who owns closing the gap; and when did we last check? If nobody can answer the second question, start with a placement check for one team.

The AI Knowledge Base is our own answer to this problem: a one-minute check places each person on a five-level path, with shared playbooks and one set of safety rules. This article describes an approach; it does not establish compliance with any law or framework.
