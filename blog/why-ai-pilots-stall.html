Why AI pilots stall before production

A pilot answers a narrow question: can this approach do something useful under the conditions we tested? Production asks a wider one: can the organization deliver that value repeatedly, at an acceptable cost, with a clear response when the system gets something wrong? The distance between those questions is where an AI program can lose its footing.

01

Start with a unit of work, not a model

Choose a business unit of measurement that survives a vendor change: an accepted proposal, a resolved service request, a reviewed contract, or an approved analysis. Name the person responsible for the outcome and record how the work happens today. Without that baseline, faster drafting can be mistaken for faster resolution, and more output can be mistaken for more value.

For a fictional Meridian service team, the target might be a shorter time to an accurate, policy-compliant response. A fluent draft is one intermediate product. If the reviewer still has to retrieve the evidence and reconstruct the answer, the work has moved rather than disappeared. Measure the complete workflow, including the people who receive its output.

02

Put human review inside the economics

The model invoice is only one cost component. Retrieval, integration, evaluation, monitoring, support, and the handling of exceptions also consume resources. Review time matters because it can recur on every task. A cheaper model that creates more correction work can be more expensive per accepted result.

The explorer above deliberately uses a simple, editable calculation: AI processing cost plus human review cost. Its numbers are illustrative assumptions, not vendor prices or predicted savings. Use it to see which variable deserves measurement. Then add the costs it excludes and compare the result against your actual baseline. Treat saved capacity and cash savings as different claims; time becomes financial benefit only through a credible change in the operation.

03

Define the production decision before the pilot ends

Agree what evidence will justify expansion before the team becomes invested in a favorable answer. Include representative cases, difficult inputs, exception handling, and checks by the people who own quality. Set a budget and latency envelope alongside the quality threshold. A system that performs well on average may still be unsuitable for a particular consequential task.

Decide who can pause the workflow, which conditions require human handling, and how a user reports a problem. The voluntary NIST AI RMF Playbook provides actions organized around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. Our practical recommendation is to turn that ongoing discipline into a short decision record: evidence reviewed, limits accepted, next owner, and next review date.

04

Make the next investment a bounded commitment

Scaling need not mean opening the capability to every department. It can mean adding one representative queue, testing another class of documents, or extending a supervised workflow to a second team. Each step should resolve a named uncertainty rather than merely increase usage.

A useful review ends with one of three decisions: expand within stated limits, revise the design and test again, or stop. All three can protect the business. The weak outcome is continued spending without a decision because the pilot remains popular. Give the owner enough authority to close that loop—and make the evidence visible to the people funding it.
