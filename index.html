FINDING 01 — ECONOMICS

Token prices fall while the actual bill rises.

Configuration choices alone — caching, batching, tokenizer settings — can move the cost of the same job 5–9× before a single line of business logic changes.

FINDING 02 — DATA EXPOSURE

Shadow AI is usually the biggest exposure channel.

Same tools your team already uses — different contract, different data terms. The one number moving the right way: personal-account use fell from 78% to 47% as governed alternatives rolled out.

FINDING 03 — VENDOR STRATEGY

You don't control the control plane.

You own the orchestration, retrieval, agent wiring, and prompts. The vendor owns pricing, deprecation, rate limits, and data-use terms — and "reserved capacity" guarantees a price, not that capacity is actually available.

FINDING 04 — ADOPTION VS. GOVERNANCE

Adoption is near-universal. Governed operation is not.

That gap is where risk and wasted money concentrate — and it's what a new AI strategy lead inherits on day one.

FINDING 05 — SECURITY

Excessive agency jumped from 6th to 3rd.

Three separable root causes — excessive functionality, permissions, and autonomy — and none of them need an attacker to trigger. Shared API keys for agents are a critical finding on their own.

FINDING 06 — RELIABILITY

Models get retired on the vendor's clock, not yours.

Most regulated workflows aren't built to survive a ~60-day window. Reproducible inference exists — but only off the hosted API, at a real cost premium.

The value gap

Getting the right answer clears one gate out of four.

Our AIR research desk audited the documented enterprise AI pilots that failed — and most of them failed with the technology working. The money leaks at four gates, and every leak traces back to an operating choice made months before a model was trained.

Gate 01

Is there value on the table?

Is the problem worth money, is the value ownable, and is the counterfactual — do nothing, fix the process, buy it — worse? Assessed before any measurement exists, so it's usually skipped.

Rarely managed
Gate 02

Does the model work?

Accuracy, latency, unit cost, reliability under the production distribution. The one gate with a mature tooling market and a clear owner — and the one that matters least often.

Well managed
Gate 03

Does the work change?

Output reaches a decision-maker at the decision moment, in a form they trust and act on. That requires process redesign, not deployment.

Partly managed
Gate 04

Does anyone collect?

Freed capacity has to be converted — headcount not hired, hours redeployed to revenue, a loss avoided. Someone must own that conversion, and it's almost never the pilot's owner.

01

Write the P&L line first.

Before any tool is evaluated: one sentence naming the account that will move, who owns it, the size of the move, and the date it's visible. If it can't be written, the pilot isn't ready.

02

Kill rate is a health metric.

Write the kill criterion into the charter with a number, a date, and a name. A program that has terminated nothing in eighteen months has stopped measuring.

03

The sponsor gives up the resource.

Make the accountable executive the one whose headcount line falls, not the one whose budget rises. It puts the strongest sceptic in charge of go/no-go.

04

Buy the workflow, build the edge.

External partnerships reached deployment about twice as often as internal builds. Build only where proprietary data or workflow depth is the advantage.
