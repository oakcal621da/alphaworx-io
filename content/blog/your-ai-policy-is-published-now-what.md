Publishing an AI policy creates a reference point. The next test is whether people can use it to make a real decision. A team wants to add a data source, enable an agent, or expand an existing pilot. Can they identify the relevant requirements, the evidence to provide, and the person authorized to decide?

This is where governance becomes an operating practice. The following approach is Alphaworx’s practical guidance; it should be adapted to the organization’s activities, authority, and applicable obligations.

## Translate the rules into work

Take a requirement such as “AI outputs must receive appropriate human review.” The operating design needs to explain which outputs, who reviews them, what the reviewer checks, and what happens when a concern is found. A checkbox confirming that a policy was read does not establish that the review can be performed.

For a customer-service drafting assistant, that might mean a service reviewer checks factual support and customer commitments before a response is sent. The team needs a route for uncertain cases and evidence that the review actually occurs. The policy states the expectation; the workflow makes it usable.

## Link requirements to the AI use

Different uses can require different controls. An internal drafting tool and a workflow that changes customer records do not have the same operating boundary. Identify the use, its owner, the information it can reach, the actions it can take, and the consequences of an error before selecting the review requirements.

Keep that applicability decision with the asset record. Include the governing policy version, relevant requirements, assigned responsibilities, and unresolved gaps. This gives a later reviewer a basis for understanding why the conditions were chosen.

## Ask for evidence that answers a question

Evidence should support the specific decision. If the concern is unauthorized retrieval, request representative allow-and-deny tests and a review of source permissions. If the concern is incorrect customer commitments, inspect examples of reviewer decisions and the escalation route. A generic architecture document may provide context without resolving either question.

Record limitations as well as findings. A successful test under one role or data set does not establish every future case. Keep the result’s scope visible and identify what would require another review.

## Keep exceptions and risk acceptance distinct

An exception to an internal control and a decision to accept residual risk answer different questions. A proposed temporary variance should identify the requirement affected, the reason, compensating measures, the responsible person, the deciding authority, and an expiry or review condition.

Recording an exception does not itself establish that the person approving it has authority or that the remaining exposure is acceptable. Resolve the relevant risk decision separately when required by the organization’s operating model. Changes in law or binding obligations require appropriate specialist judgment; a workflow approval cannot replace it.

## Give change an explicit route back to review

A new connector, supplier change, expanded user group, or different permitted action can alter the basis of an earlier decision. Define the changes that trigger reassessment and assign the person responsible for noticing them. Agree what can continue while the review is open.

A regular governance meeting should inspect unresolved decisions, expiring exceptions, incidents, and changes that affect existing approvals. The useful output is a set of recorded decisions and owned actions, followed at the next review.

## Try this at the next leadership review

Select one AI workflow and ask: which policy version applies; who owns its controls; what evidence supports the current boundary; what remains unresolved; and which change would send it back for review? If answers conflict, assign the gap and a next step before treating the workflow as settled.
