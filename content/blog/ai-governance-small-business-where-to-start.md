A small business can begin governing AI without creating a separate department. Start with the work people already do: drafting proposals, summarizing meetings, searching information, or answering customers. Establish enough structure to know what is happening and who will respond when something needs attention.

The right starting point depends on the task, information, and possible consequences. A small team handling sensitive or consequential work may need specialist review from the outset. Organizational size alone does not settle the design.

## Make a usable inventory

Ask your team which AI tools and embedded features they use, what work they support, and what information they submit or retrieve. Include personal accounts and trial tools in the discovery conversation. Treat uncertainty as something to resolve, so people have a reason to give a complete account.

For each use, record a plain-language purpose, tool or provider, responsible person, information involved, and current status. Identify whether it only prepares material or can send messages, change records, or perform another action. A short, maintained register is more useful than a detailed inventory nobody owns.

## Agree the boundaries for information and action

Choose the accounts and tools the business has reviewed for the intended work. Check the actual service terms, settings, sharing arrangements, and information access with the right provider or adviser. A familiar brand does not establish that every account or integration is appropriate.

Tell people which information may be used, when permission is needed, and what to do if they are unsure. For a proposal-drafting assistant, the boundary might initially exclude confidential client material and require a person to review every externally shared draft. Record those conditions and the basis for them; the example is a starting design, not a universal rule.

## Put a person behind the outcome

Name an owner for each use. That person needs to understand the task, maintain its record, and know how to seek help. One person can hold several roles in a small business, but high-consequence decisions may still need independent review or specialist input.

Make review concrete. For a research summary, what sources should be checked? For customer-facing wording, who checks accuracy and commitments? If the reviewer lacks time or access to the evidence, revise the workflow before assuming review will protect the outcome.

## Choose one opportunity you can evaluate

Select a bounded task with an observable baseline. For example, a services firm could test drafting an initial proposal outline using approved material. Measure preparation time, reviewer effort, missing content, and corrections. Keep the reviewer’s decision to accept or reject a draft visible.

Include subscriptions, setup, and review effort in the cost picture. Time released can be useful capacity, but it is not automatically a cash saving. Agree what evidence would justify continuing, changing the design, or stopping the experiment before expanding it.

## Create a simple route for problems and changes

Give staff a named contact and a clear way to report a wrong output, unintended sharing, or an action they did not expect. Establish who can pause the workflow and how the business will determine whether it can restart. Use existing incident and escalation arrangements where they fit.

Review the register and open actions on a manageable cadence. Revisit a use when its provider, data access, audience, or permitted actions change. A new connector can matter more than another month of ordinary usage.

## Bring five questions to your first review

What AI is in use? Who owns each outcome? What information and actions are permitted? Who checks the work? What evidence will determine the next step? Start with one workflow and record any unknowns with an owner and a date to resolve them.
